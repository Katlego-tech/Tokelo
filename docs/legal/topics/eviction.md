A tenant must not be evicted without a court order (Gauteng's Unfair Practices Regulations, regulation 9(1)). The Rental Housing Act gives a landlord the right to repossess the property when a lease ends only after obtaining a court order (section 4(5)(d)). Where a tenant breaches the lease, a landlord who wants to deprive the tenant of access to or full use of the dwelling must give seven days' notice to put the breach right, unless the tenant has been in default of rent for seven days after it was due, and must obtain a court order to evict (regulation 9(5)).

Under the PIE Act, the court must serve written notice of eviction proceedings at least 14 days before the hearing. The notice must give the date and time, the grounds, and say that the occupier may appear and defend the case and, where necessary, apply for legal aid. The court may order an eviction only if it is just and equitable, after considering all the relevant circumstances, including the rights and needs of the elderly, children, disabled persons and households headed by women. Where the occupation has lasted more than six months, those circumstances also include whether land has been or can reasonably be made available for the occupier's relocation, except after a sale in execution under a mortgage. The court sets the date by which the occupier must leave (section 4). No one may evict an unlawful occupier without a court order, and doing so is an offence (section 8).

From the day a complaint is lodged with the Rental Housing Tribunal until it rules, or for three months if that is sooner, the landlord may not evict the tenant, and the tenant must keep paying the rent. The Tribunal itself does not hear eviction applications (Rental Housing Act, section 13(7) and 13(14)).
